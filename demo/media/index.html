Broadcast media and sound change

An interactive presentation of Rathcke, Castellano & Canzi (2024), Understanding the role of broadcast media in sound change, using the study’s final data.

demos

  • ICPhS · 2023 Face masks and the perception of fricatives Do face masks make speech harder to understand because they muffle the sound, or because they hide the lips?
  • Speech Dynamics · 2024 Broadcast media and sound change Can two weeks of watching an American or a British TV series change how German learners pronounce English?

The role of broadcast media in sound change is widely debated, and direct evidence is scarce. This study tested whether television exposure can change the pronunciation of second-language speakers in a setting where media are the main source of native-speaker input: German secondary-school students learning English.

18German students in their final year of secondary school, B2-level English, mean age 17
2 seriesAtypical (American) or The End of the F***ing World (British), assigned at random
14 daysone episode a day, in the original English, without subtitles
68 wordsread aloud before and after: 51 with a post-vocalic /r/, 17 with an intervocalic /t/

Linguistic variables

The study looked at two variables. Most American English accents are rhotic, realising post-vocalic /r/ (e.g. car, nurse, letter), whereas most accents of England are non-rhotic. In American English, intervocalic /t/ after a stressed vowel is also realised as a flap [ɾ] (e.g. water, better), known as /t/-flapping; British English typically has [t] or a glottal stop. Each token was coded for the presence (1) or absence (0) of the American variant.

Results: media exposure

watched Atypical (American) watched TEOTFW (British)

Raw percentage of American variants (flapped /t/, pronounced /r/) in the final data. The chapter reports model-predicted probabilities from mixed-effects logistic regression, which differ somewhat.

/t/-flapping increased significantly in the group exposed to the American series; the decrease in the group exposed to the British series was not significant. Rhoticity changed in the expected direction in both groups, but the interaction of group and exposure was not significant. The chapter suggests that the difference between the two variables may be related to /t/-flapping being a more “off-the-shelf” variant than rhoticity.

Results: involvement with the series

Three individual measures were tested: English aptitude, engagement (regularity and frequency of viewing) and emotional involvement with the series. Only involvement explained variation in the data. For rhoticity, the three-way interaction of group, exposure and involvement approached significance (χ² = 3.81, p = .051); in a combined model of both variables, it was significant (χ² = 4.72, p = .02).

The plot below shows each participant’s change in the proportion of American variants against their involvement score. If involvement moderates accommodation, the slope should be positive in the American-series group and negative in the British-series group. Points can be moved or removed to show how much a single participant affects a slope with nine participants per group. These per-participant regressions are for illustration only; the inferential analyses in the chapter use mixed-effects logistic regression.

Reference
